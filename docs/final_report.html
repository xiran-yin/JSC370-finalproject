Predicting TTC Subway Delays from Weather Data

JSC370 Final Project Report

Author

Xiran Yin

Published

April 26, 2026

1 Introduction

The Toronto Transit Commission (TTC) carries over one million riders per day, making subway reliability a substantial piece of urban infrastructure. While many causes of delay — equipment failures, passenger incidents, security investigations — are random from a forecasting perspective, weather is one of the few systematic, externally observable, and forecastable variables that plausibly drives day-to-day delay patterns. Cold snaps freeze track switches; heavy snow buries third rails; extreme heat causes rail steel to expand. This project asks whether daily Toronto weather predicts TTC delays, by integrating 12 years of meteorological observations (2014–2025) with the City of Toronto’s complete public record of subway delays.

1.1 Central Research Question

Can daily weather conditions predict TTC subway delays, and if so, which weather features matter most — both for explaining day-to-day variation and for forecasting severe delay days?

The question has two halves: an explanatory half (which weather variables associate with delay, and how?) and a predictive half (can that association be turned into a forecasting tool?). Each maps to a formal hypothesis with an explicit null.

H₁ — Explanatory. Adverse weather is associated with longer total daily delays, with potentially non-linear functional form. Null: the partial slopes on precipitation and snowfall are zero, and the smooths are flat, after controlling for month and day-of-week. Alternative: slopes are positive; smooths exhibit non-linear structure (e.g., U-shaped temperature). H₁ is tested with OLS and a Generalized Additive Model (GAM).

H₂ — Predictive. A supervised ML model trained on weather and temporal features outperforms a no-information baseline. Null: out-of-sample R² ≤ 0 and ROC-AUC ≤ 0.5. Alternative: R² > 0 and ROC-AUC > 0.5 on a chronologically held-out test set. H₂ is tested by training Linear/Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost on 2014–2023 data and evaluating on 2024–2025.

GitHub repo: https://github.com/xiran-yin/JSC370-finalproject.

2 Methods

2.1 Data Sources

Two independent sources are combined: (1) Weather — Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, Toronto (43.7064°N, −79.3986°W), daily aggregates 2014–2026; (2) TTC delays — City of Toronto Open Data Portal ttc-subway-delay-data, every recorded incident 2014–2025, retrieved via the CKAN API.

2.2 Data Acquisition and Cleaning

Weather acquisition is one HTTPS GET to the Open-Meteo archive. TTC acquisition handles three obstacles: (i) format dispatching (mixed CSV/XLSX requires different pandas readers); (ii) multi-sheet Excel (2014–2017 files split records across 12 monthly worksheets — sheet_name=None is required to avoid silently dropping ~92% of those years); (iii) inconsistent column names (“Date” / “Occurrence Date”; “Min Delay” / “min_delay”) — handled by substring-matching against known synonyms.

The merged dataset has 4,384 daily observations. A three-level categorical weather severity variable is derived using two fixed breakpoints — precip_mm ≥ 10 or snowfall_cm ≥ 5 for “Heavy”, either positive but neither at threshold for “Light”, otherwise “Clear” — corresponding to Environment Canada’s heavy-rain advisory level. Because the TTC has experienced a structural rise in delays since 2021 (mean total daily delay rose from ~113 min/day in 2014 to ~200 min/day by 2022), we detrend the predictive target by subtracting each day’s calendar-month mean. The detrended target delay_anomaly represents how much worse (or better) a given day was than typical for that month, isolating weather-driven variation from baseline drift. The binary classification target is_severe flags days at or above the training-period 75th percentile of delay_anomaly, so that “severe” means the same thing across the 12-year window.

2.3 Statistical and Machine-Learning Methods

Inferential (testing H₁): (i) OLS multiple regression of total_delay_mins on precipitation, snowfall, gust speed, mean temperature, weekend, and month fixed effects, as a linear baseline. (ii) GAM via pygam.LinearGAM(s(precip) + s(temp) + f(weekend) + f(month)), with 20-knot penalized cubic splines and smoothing parameters chosen by generalized cross-validation, to capture non-linearity.

Predictive (testing H₂): three supervised models fit twice — once for regression (target: delay_anomaly) and once for classification (target: is_severe). The models are Linear/Logistic Regression (baseline), Random Forest (sklearn.ensemble), and XGBoost (xgboost). Hyperparameters: RF — n_estimators=300–400, max_depth=8–10; XGBoost — n_estimators=400, max_depth=6, learning_rate=0.05, subsample=0.8. Twelve features are used: precip_mm, snowfall_cm, precip_hours, precip_intensity, temp_max, temp_min, mean_temp_c, temp_range, gust_speed, is_weekend, month_num, dow_num. The data are split chronologically — train on 2014-01-01 to 2023-12-31, test on 2024-01-01 onward — to prevent the model from learning future information. Regression metrics: RMSE, MAE, R². Classification metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC.

System-wide total delay averages 144 min/day with a heavy right tail. Mean total delay rises monotonically from Clear (~137 min) to Light (~146 min) to Heavy (~179 min), consistent with severe events triggering disproportionate disruption.

OLS shows three results. (i) Precipitation and snowfall are both significant positive predictors; snowfall’s per-unit effect (~14 min/cm) is roughly 10× larger than rain’s (~1.2 min/mm) — frozen precipitation is operationally far more disruptive. (ii) Weekends lose ~32 fewer minutes to delay than weather-matched weekdays. (iii) Mean temperature is non-significant linearly — an artifact, since the GAM below shows a U-shape that a single linear coefficient cannot capture.

The GAM (pseudo-R² = 0.063) rejects H₁₀. Two non-linearities OLS cannot capture: (a) temperature is U-shaped (Figure 1); (b) the precipitation smooth saturates near ~10 mm — its right-edge downturn beyond ~15 mm is an extrapolation artifact in a sparse data region, not real saturation. Combined with the OLS coefficients, the explanatory null is rejected across multiple specifications.

3.3 H₂ — Predictive Models

After detrending, XGBoost achieves the best out-of-sample performance for both tasks (regression: RMSE = 113.2 min, R² = 0.077; classification: ROC-AUC = 0.538). Random Forest is close behind; linear models lag substantially. All three classifiers clear the 0.5 baseline, so H₂₀ is rejected. The 90th-percentile sensitivity check yields a comparable XGBoost AUC of 0.581, confirming robustness to the cutoff choice. The R² is modest in absolute terms, as expected — most large single-day delays are caused by random non-weather events (signal failures, security incidents) unforecastable from weather alone. The top three features by gain — snowfall, mean temperature, day-of-week (Figure 3) — match the OLS and GAM findings, providing convergent evidence across linear, spline-based, and tree-based model classes.

4 Conclusions and Summary

H₁ — Explanatory (rejected the null). OLS finds precipitation (β ≈ 1.2 min/mm, p < 0.001) and snowfall (β ≈ 14 min/cm, p < 0.001) as significant positive predictors; snowfall’s per-unit effect is roughly an order of magnitude larger than rain’s. The GAM further reveals a U-shaped temperature effect — predicted delay minimized 5–20°C and rising at both extremes — that OLS could not capture. The weekend indicator is strongly negative (β ≈ −32 min, p < 0.001).

H₂ — Predictive (rejected the null). After detrending the target to remove the post-2021 baseline rise, XGBoost achieves the best out-of-sample performance for both regression (RMSE ≈ 113 min, R² ≈ 0.08) and classification (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.54). All three models clear the no-information baselines, and the 90th-percentile sensitivity check confirms the conclusion does not depend on the cutoff. Most importantly, the top three features by gain — snowfall, mean temperature, day-of-week — match the predictors that OLS and GAM identified as most consequential, demonstrating convergence between explanatory and predictive analyses across very different model classes.

4.1 Note on Detrending and Distribution Shift

A direct prediction of total_delay_mins produces negative out-of-sample R² for all three models — not because weather lacks predictive signal, but because the test-period mean total delay (~200 min) is far above the training-period mean (~140 min), so any model anchored to the training distribution systematically under-predicts. By predicting delay_anomaly (each day’s deviation from its calendar-month mean) we remove this baseline drift and isolate the weather-driven component. This is the methodologically appropriate approach when the target series is non-stationary, as is the case for TTC delays after 2021.

4.2 Limitations

  1. Weather explains a modest fraction of variance. Even after detrending, XGBoost achieves only R² ≈ 0.08. Most day-to-day variation in delay anomalies is driven by non-weather factors (equipment failures, security incidents, passenger emergencies) the data do not capture. Operational data (rolling-stock age, ridership) at daily granularity would likely improve performance but is not publicly available. (2) Aggregation hides line-level heterogeneity. Different lines have different vulnerabilities — Line 1 (mostly underground) is largely insulated from snow, while at-grade segments are far more weather-sensitive. Line/station disaggregation is a natural next step. (3) Production deployment requires re-training. The post-2021 baseline rise documented here means a model trained on historical data cannot be deployed indefinitely; annual or quarterly re-training would be needed to track the drifting baseline.

# Methods

## Data Sources

Two independent sources are combined: (1) **Weather** — [Open-Meteo Historical Weather API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api), Toronto (43.7064°N, −79.3986°W), daily aggregates 2014–2026; (2) **TTC delays** — City of Toronto Open Data Portal [`ttc-subway-delay-data`](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/ttc-subway-delay-data/), every recorded incident 2014–2025, retrieved via the CKAN API.

## Data Acquisition and Cleaning

```{python}
#| label: weather

start_date, end_date = "2014-01-01", "2026-01-01"
url = (
    f"https://archive-api.open-meteo.com/v1/archive?"
    f"latitude=43.7064&longitude=-79.3986&start_date={start_date}&end_date={end_date}"
    f"&daily=temperature_2m_max,temperature_2m_min,apparent_temperature_max,"
    f"precipitation_sum,snowfall_sum,wind_speed_10m_max,wind_gusts_10m_max,"
    f"precipitation_hours&timezone=America%2FNew_York"
)
w = requests.get(url).json()['daily']
df_weather = pd.DataFrame({
    'date': pd.to_datetime(w['time']),
    'temp_max': w['temperature_2m_max'], 'temp_min': w['temperature_2m_min'],
    'precip_mm': w['precipitation_sum'], 'precip_hours': w['precipitation_hours'],
    'snowfall_cm': w['snowfall_sum'],
    'gust_speed': w['wind_gusts_10m_max']
})
df_weather['precip_intensity'] = df_weather.apply(
    lambda x: x['precip_mm']/x['precip_hours'] if x['precip_hours']>0 else 0, axis=1
)
```

```{python}
#| label: ttc

base_url = "https://ckan0.cf.opendata.inter.prod-toronto.ca/api/3/action/package_show"
headers = {'User-Agent': 'Mozilla/5.0'}
response = requests.get(base_url, params={"id": "ttc-subway-delay-data"}, headers=headers)
all_data = []

if response.status_code == 200:
    for res in response.json()["result"]["resources"]:
        fmt = res["format"].upper()
        if fmt in ["CSV","XLSX","XLS"]:
            try:
                file_res = requests.get(res["url"], headers=headers, timeout=30)
                if fmt == "CSV":
                    df_temp = pd.read_csv(
                        io.StringIO(file_res.content.decode('utf-8', errors='ignore')),
                        low_memory=False)
                else:
                    excel_dict = pd.read_excel(io.BytesIO(file_res.content), sheet_name=None)
                    df_temp = pd.concat(excel_dict.values(), ignore_index=True)
                date_cands = ['date','occ_date','occurrence date','report date']
                delay_cands = ['delay','min_delay','min delay','minimum delay']
                fd = next((c for c in df_temp.columns if any(x in c.lower() for x in date_cands)), None)
                fdel = next((c for c in df_temp.columns if any(x in c.lower() for x in delay_cands)), None)
                if fd and fdel:
                    df_temp = df_temp.rename(columns={fd: 'date', fdel: 'min_delay'})
                    df_temp['date'] = pd.to_datetime(df_temp['date'], errors='coerce')
                    all_data.append(df_temp[['date','min_delay']].dropna(subset=['date']))
            except Exception:
                pass

df_ttc_raw = pd.concat(all_data, ignore_index=True)
df_ttc_daily = df_ttc_raw.groupby('date').agg(
    total_delay_mins=('min_delay','sum'),
    total_incidents =('min_delay','count')).reset_index()
```

TTC acquisition handles three obstacles: (i) **format dispatching** (mixed CSV/XLSX requires different pandas readers); (ii) **multi-sheet Excel** (2014–2017 files split records across 12 monthly worksheets — `sheet_name=None` is required to avoid silently dropping ~92% of those years); (iii) **inconsistent column names** ("Date" / "Occurrence Date"; "Min Delay" / "min_delay") — handled by substring-matching against known synonyms.

```{python}
#| label: merge

df_merged = pd.merge(df_ttc_daily, df_weather, on='date', how='inner')
df_merged['month_num']     = df_merged['date'].dt.month
df_merged['month']         = df_merged['date'].dt.month_name()
df_merged['dow_num']       = df_merged['date'].dt.dayofweek
df_merged['is_weekend']    = (df_merged['dow_num'] >= 5).astype(int)
df_merged['mean_temp_c']   = (df_merged['temp_max']+df_merged['temp_min'])/2
df_merged['temp_range']    = df_merged['temp_max']-df_merged['temp_min']

def classify_weather(row):
    if row['precip_mm']>=10 or row['snowfall_cm']>=5: return 'Heavy Rain/Snow'
    elif row['precip_mm']>0 or row['snowfall_cm']>0: return 'Light Rain/Snow'
    else: return 'Clear'
df_merged['weather_type'] = df_merged.apply(classify_weather, axis=1)
df_merged['weather_type'] = pd.Categorical(
    df_merged['weather_type'],
    categories=['Clear','Light Rain/Snow','Heavy Rain/Snow'], ordered=True)
df_merged['month_cat'] = pd.Categorical(df_merged['month'])
df_merged = df_merged.sort_values('date').reset_index(drop=True)

# Detrending: subtract calendar-month mean to remove the post-2021 baseline rise.
# Models predict the *anomaly* (deviation from the month's mean) rather than the
# absolute level, isolating the weather signal from non-stationary trend.
df_merged['month_year'] = df_merged['date'].dt.to_period('M')
month_means = df_merged.groupby('month_year')['total_delay_mins'].transform('mean')
df_merged['delay_anomaly'] = df_merged['total_delay_mins'] - month_means

# Severe-day target uses the training-period 75th percentile of the anomaly
train_mask = df_merged['date'] < pd.Timestamp('2024-01-01')
anomaly_q3 = df_merged.loc[train_mask, 'delay_anomaly'].quantile(0.75)
df_merged['is_severe'] = (df_merged['delay_anomaly'] >= anomaly_q3).astype(int)

n_obs = df_merged.shape[0]
mean_delay_2014 = df_merged[df_merged['date'].dt.year==2014]['total_delay_mins'].mean()
mean_delay_2022 = df_merged[df_merged['date'].dt.year==2022]['total_delay_mins'].mean()
```

X_gam = df_merged[['precip_mm','mean_temp_c','is_weekend','month_num']].values
y_gam = df_merged['total_delay_mins'].values
gam = LinearGAM(s(0) + s(1) + f(2) + f(3)).fit(X_gam, y_gam)
gam_r2 = gam.statistics_['pseudo_r2']['explained_deviance']

x_range = np.linspace(X_gam[:,1].min(), X_gam[:,1].max(), 200)

# Conclusions and Summary

**H₁ — Explanatory (rejected the null).** OLS finds precipitation (β ≈ 1.2 min/mm, p < 0.001) and snowfall (β ≈ 14 min/cm, p < 0.001) as significant positive predictors; snowfall's per-unit effect is roughly an order of magnitude larger than rain's. The GAM further reveals a **U-shaped temperature effect** — predicted delay minimized 5–20°C and rising at both extremes — that OLS could not capture. The weekend indicator is strongly negative (β ≈ −32 min, p < 0.001).

**H₂ — Predictive (rejected the null).** After detrending the target to remove the post-2021 baseline rise, **XGBoost achieves the best out-of-sample performance** for both regression (RMSE ≈ `{python} f"{xgb_rmse:.0f}"` min, R² ≈ `{python} f"{xgb_r2:.2f}"`) and classification (ROC-AUC ≈ `{python} f"{xgb_auc:.2f}"`). All three models clear the no-information baselines, and the 90th-percentile sensitivity check confirms the conclusion does not depend on the cutoff. Most importantly, the **top three features by gain — snowfall, mean temperature, day-of-week** — match the predictors that OLS and GAM identified as most consequential, demonstrating convergence between explanatory and predictive analyses across very different model classes.

## Note on Detrending and Distribution Shift

A direct prediction of `total_delay_mins` produces *negative* out-of-sample R² for all three models — not because weather lacks predictive signal, but because the test-period mean total delay (~200 min) is far above the training-period mean (~140 min), so any model anchored to the training distribution systematically under-predicts. By predicting `delay_anomaly` (each day's deviation from its calendar-month mean) we remove this baseline drift and isolate the weather-driven component. This is the methodologically appropriate approach when the target series is non-stationary, as is the case for TTC delays after 2021.

## Limitations

(1) **Weather explains a modest fraction of variance.** Even after detrending, XGBoost achieves only R² ≈ `{python} f"{xgb_r2:.2f}"`. Most day-to-day variation in delay anomalies is driven by non-weather factors (equipment failures, security incidents, passenger emergencies) the data do not capture. Operational data (rolling-stock age, ridership) at daily granularity would likely improve performance but is not publicly available. (2) **Aggregation hides line-level heterogeneity.** Different lines have different vulnerabilities — Line 1 (mostly underground) is largely insulated from snow, while at-grade segments are far more weather-sensitive. Line/station disaggregation is a natural next step. (3) **Production deployment requires re-training.** The post-2021 baseline rise documented here means a model trained on historical data cannot be deployed indefinitely; annual or quarterly re-training would be needed to track the drifting baseline.
